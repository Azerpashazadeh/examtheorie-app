Vulnerable Road Users

Vulnerable road users include pedestrians, but also the blind, partially sighted, and persons with limited mobility.

Pedestrian Crossing

At a pedestrian crossing (also known as a zebra crossing) you, as a driver, are obliged to give way to pedestrians who cross or want to cross. You should also let drivers of a disabled vehicle who move from one pavement or footpath to another via a pedestrian crossing go first. In that case they belong to the pedestrian category.

Make it clear that you plan to stop. Do not brake at the last minute. Release your gas in time and brake lightly. This way you let the traffic behind you know that you are going to brake and maybe stop.

⚠ If there are traffic lights at the crossing, the traffic lights take precedence over the traffic signs. In that case, pedestrians simply have to wait for the light to turn green.

People with Limited Mobility and the Blind

If there is no pedestrian crossing, you do not have to let a pedestrian who wants to cross the road go first. However, there are two exceptions:

  • Blind or visually impaired persons who can be recognised by a blind stick (white cane with one or more red rings).
  • Persons with difficulty moving who can be recognised by, for example, a walker, a cane, or crutches.

These two groups should be given priority if they want to cross, no matter where they do this. Persons travelling by wheelchair are not covered by this exception — they are not covered by the term "persons with difficulty moving".

🦯
Blind / Visually Impaired ✔

White cane with one or more red rings. Must be given priority anywhere on the road.

🚶
Persons with Difficulty Moving ✔

Recognisable by a walker, cane, or crutches. Must be given priority anywhere on the road.

♿
Wheelchair User ✘

Not covered by "persons with difficulty moving". No automatic priority outside a pedestrian crossing.

📍
No Location Restriction

Priority for blind persons and those with difficulty moving applies no matter where they want to cross.

Entrance and Exit Constructions

An entrance construction is the same as an exit construction — which word you use depends on whether you enter or exit such a construction.

Entrance and exit constructions are used at the beginning and end of an erf, and also at the entrance of a parking lot. Most are designed so that you drive over the pavement; the pavement tiles continue and the kerb has been lowered at the height of the construction to make it easier to drive over.

With entrance and exit constructions, you must let all other traffic, including pedestrians, go first — both when entering and exiting. When one driver wants to enter and another wants to exit at the same time, it is preferable to give way to the outgoing driver so you can enter more easily.

Normally, an erf has an entrance and exit designed as an entrance or exit construction. In that case, you must give way to all other traffic when entering and leaving. If the entrance or exit construction is missing, the normal priority rules apply.

Erf

The rule that you have to give way to all traffic has nothing to do with the erf itself — it only applies at the entrance and exit construction.

End of Erf

💡 Because most entrance and exit constructions are not very wide, it is preferred to give way to the outgoing driver first when there is a conflict — this makes it easier for you to enter.
