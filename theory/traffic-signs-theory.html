Traffic Signs

Road signs, traffic lights and road surface markings — what they mean, which ones override which, and how to read every signal on a Dutch road.

  • General Rules
  • Road Signs
  • Traffic Lights
  • Road Markings

Chapter 3 covers everything you see on the road that is not another vehicle. Signs on poles, lights at intersections, lines and symbols painted on the asphalt — all of these are traffic signs in the legal sense, and all of them carry binding instructions for road users.

Understanding how signs work — which ones take priority, how they interact with each other, and what each colour and shape communicates — is as fundamental to the Dutch driving theory exam as the traffic rules themselves.

§ 1 — General Rules for Traffic Signs

The foundational rules that apply to all traffic signs — signs on poles, lights, and markings on the road surface alike.

Obligation to Obey All road users

Every road user must obey all traffic signs that apply to them. This is not optional. A sign displayed according to the rules is legally binding from the moment a road user can reasonably see it.

This obligation applies to the road user themselves — not just to the driver of a vehicle. Pedestrians, cyclists, moped riders, horse riders — all must obey the signs that address them.

Signs Override General Rules Priority of signs

A traffic sign always overrides the general traffic rules of the road. If the general rule says give way to the right, but a sign indicates you have priority — the sign wins. Signs are specific instructions for a specific location; the general rules are defaults that apply only when no sign says otherwise.

Variable Message Signs Electronic

Variable message signs — electronic signs that can change their display — carry the same legal force as fixed signs. This includes the matrix signs above motorways that display speed limits, lane closures (red X) or open lanes (green arrow). When a matrix sign shows a red cross above a lane, that lane is closed and driving in it is prohibited. When it shows a speed limit, that limit applies immediately.

A blank matrix sign — one showing nothing — does not change the applicable rules. The default rules and any fixed signs below continue to apply.
Supplementary Boards (Onderborden) Under the sign

A supplementary board is a smaller panel placed directly below a traffic sign. It modifies, limits or clarifies the main sign. For example, a no-parking sign may have a supplementary board showing specific hours during which parking is prohibited — outside those hours, parking is permitted. Always read supplementary boards as part of the main sign instruction.

Where Signs Apply Scope of a sign

A traffic sign generally applies from the point where it is placed. Signs for a road apply to the entire carriageway in the direction indicated. Some signs apply only to the lane above which they are positioned — this is the case for matrix signs on motorways. Supplementary boards can further define the zone of application.

The Authority Hierarchy

When instructions conflict, this order always applies — top overrides everything below it.

§ 2 — Road Signs (Verkeersborden)

Dutch road signs are organised into categories by letter code. Shape and colour communicate the type of instruction before you even read the detail.

How Dutch Signs Are Organised

Every Dutch road sign belongs to a lettered category. The category tells you immediately what kind of instruction the sign carries. Learning the categories is one of the most efficient ways to prepare for the theory exam — once you know the system, individual signs become much easier to recognise and remember.

Priority Signs — Category B

B6 vs B7 — Give Way vs Stop

The B6 (give way) requires you to yield — slow down, check, and proceed only if safe. You do not have to stop if the way is clear. The B7 (stop) requires a complete stop — you must come to a full standstill before the stop line, regardless of whether traffic is present. Failing to make a full stop at a B7 is a violation even if no other vehicle is visible.

Reading Warning Signs Correctly

Warning signs do not change your right of way or prohibit any action. They warn you to prepare. When you see a J-sign, reduce speed, increase following distance, and be ready for the hazard described. The sign tells you what is coming — it is your job to respond appropriately.

Speed Limit Signs — Ending a Limit

A3 — End of Speed Limit

A round sign with a strikethrough over a number ends the specific speed limit shown. After this sign, the default speed limit for that road type applies again. If you are on a motorway and the A3 follows an A1-70 sign, you return to 130 km/h.

A4 — Advisory Speed

A square sign (not round) with a speed number is an advisory speed — recommended, not mandatory. It typically appears before a sharp bend or road narrowing. You are not breaking the law by going faster, but the advisory exists for a reason.

§ 3 — Traffic Lights (Verkeerslichten)

What each light colour and combination means — and what you must do when you see it.

Obligation to Obey Traffic Lights All road users

Traffic lights are legally binding. Every road user must follow the instruction of the light that applies to them. Running a red light is one of the most serious traffic violations and is a common cause of fatal accidents at intersections.

If traffic lights are not functioning — showing nothing at all — the general traffic rules apply, including the give-way-to-the-right rule at intersections.

Standard Vehicle Traffic Lights

Red — Stop
Come to a full stop before the stop line. Do not proceed under any circumstances.
Red + Yellow — Prepare
Prepare to move off. Do not proceed yet — green has not shown.
Green — Proceed
You may proceed, but still give way to pedestrians and cyclists already crossing.
Yellow — Stop if safe
Stop before the line if you can do so safely. Only proceed if stopping would be dangerous.
→
Green Arrow — Conditional go
You may proceed in the direction of the arrow only. Give way to all other traffic crossing your path.
Flashing Yellow — Caution
No binding stop instruction. Proceed with care, apply general traffic rules.
Yellow Light — The Most Misunderstood Signal

Yellow does not mean accelerate. It means stop — if you can do so safely. The exception is if stopping would require sudden harsh braking that could cause a rear-end collision or other danger. Only in that case may you continue through yellow. The burden of proof is always on the driver who passed the line on yellow.

Green — Not Unconditional

A green light permits you to proceed — but it does not remove your obligation to give way. When a light turns green, you must still yield to pedestrians who are crossing and cyclists who are on the crossing. Green means you may go — it does not mean everything in your path must stop for you.

Pedestrian Traffic Lights

Red figure — Do not cross
Wait at the kerb. Do not step onto the crossing.
Green figure — Cross now
Cross promptly. If the green starts flashing, complete the crossing — do not start if you are not yet on the road.
Flashing green figure — Hurry
Finish crossing. Do not start crossing if you have not yet stepped off the kerb.

Bicycle Traffic Lights

Bicycle-Specific Lights Fietsers

Where a separate traffic light for cyclists is present — recognisable by the bicycle symbol on the light housing — cyclists must follow that light, not the general vehicle light. The same red/yellow/green rules apply.

Where no separate bicycle light is present, cyclists follow the general vehicle traffic lights.

Some intersections have a small supplementary green arrow light for cyclists that allows them to proceed in a specific direction while vehicle traffic is stopped. This is the cyclist's equivalent of the conditional green arrow for cars.

Tram & Bus Lights

Public Transport Traffic Lights Trams & buses

Trams and buses often have their own traffic lights — typically showing white symbols (vertical bar = stop; horizontal bar = proceed; diagonal bar = turn). These signals apply only to public transport vehicles and are not addressed to other road users. Car drivers should ignore tram/bus signal lights.

Matrix Signs Above Motorway Lanes

Lane Signals on Motorways Per-lane control

On Dutch motorways, electronic matrix signs above each lane control that specific lane individually. The possible signals are:

  • Green arrow pointing down — lane is open, you may use it
  • Red cross (X) — lane is closed, you must not drive in it
  • Yellow arrow pointing left or right — move to the adjacent lane; your current lane is about to close
  • Speed limit number — that specific speed limit applies in this lane
A matrix sign overrides the general speed limit for that lane. If the general motorway limit is 130 km/h but the matrix shows 90 km/h, you must drive at 90 km/h in that lane.

§ 4 — Road Surface Markings

Lines, symbols and patterns on the road surface — what each one means and how to respond to it.

Road Markings Are Binding Legal force

Road surface markings carry the same legal force as signs on poles. A continuous white line, shark's teeth, a stop line — all are legally binding instructions. Driving over a solid continuous line or ignoring a stop line is a violation, just as running a red light would be.

Lane Lines

Continuous White Line — Lane Boundary

A solid continuous white line between lanes may not be crossed. This applies to the centre line between opposing traffic directions and to lane-dividing lines marked as continuous. You must stay on your side.

Broken White Line — May Cross

A broken (dashed) white line marks a lane boundary that may be crossed when safe to do so — for example, when overtaking or changing lanes. You must still check that it is safe before crossing.

Double Continuous Line — Absolutely No Crossing

A double solid white line is an absolute prohibition. Neither side may cross it. This marking is used on roads where overtaking or lane changes would be especially dangerous — for example, approaching the crest of a hill.

Continuous + Broken Combination

When a continuous line runs alongside a broken line, the rule depends on which side you are on. If the continuous line is on your side, you may not cross. If the broken line is on your side, you may cross when safe. Look at the line closest to you.

Stop and Give-Way Lines

Stop Line (Stopstreep)

A thick continuous white line across the road at a traffic light or stop sign. You must stop with the front of your vehicle behind this line. No part of your vehicle may cross the line while stopped.

Give-Way Line (Haaientanden)

Shark's teeth — rows of triangles pointing toward you — indicate a give-way obligation. You must yield to traffic on the road you are about to enter. Often found at roundabouts and at junctions where a B6 sign is also present.

Pedestrian & Cyclist Crossings

Zebra Crossing (Voetgangersoversteekplaats)

Parallel white stripes across the road. Drivers must give way to pedestrians who are on the crossing or who are clearly about to step onto it. Cyclists also have priority at combined pedestrian/cyclist crossings where a bicycle symbol is added.

Cyclist Crossing (Fietsoversteekplaats)

Marked with two broken white lines across the road and a bicycle symbol. Cyclists have priority here if combined with a B6 sign or shark's teeth. Without those, the general priority rules apply.

Special Marked Zones on the Road Surface

Block Markings (Blokmarkering)

A pattern of white blocks separates an entry or exit lane from the main carriageway. Driving over block markings is not automatically prohibited, but they indicate a transition zone — treat with care.

Diversion Area (Verdrijvingsvlak)

An area covered with diagonal stripes. Driving on this is prohibited. It is used to steer traffic away from an obstacle or to separate opposing streams. Treat it as solid ground you must not enter.

Junction Point (Puntstuk)

A triangular or polygonal marked area at a point where lanes split or merge. Do not drive on it and do not stop on it. It is a visual guide to keep traffic separated at complex junctions.

Yellow Lines — Stopping Restrictions

A single yellow line along the kerb indicates a stopping restriction during specific hours shown on a supplementary board. A double yellow line (or a solid yellow kerb line) means no stopping at any time.

BUS / LIJNBUS Text

The word "BUS" or "LIJNBUS" painted on the road surface marks a bus lane or bus road. Only authorised vehicles — buses, and sometimes taxis and cyclists per supplementary sign — may use this lane.

Bicycle Symbol on Carriageway

A bicycle image painted on the road marks a bicycle lane (fietsstrook). Combined with a solid line: other drivers must not enter. Combined with a broken line: other drivers may enter briefly if needed.

Continuous vs Broken — The Key Distinction

Across Dutch road markings, the continuous/broken distinction is fundamental. Continuous = may not cross. Broken = may cross when safe. This applies to lane lines, bicycle lane borders, and bus lane borders. When in doubt, if the line is solid, treat it as a wall.

Arrow Markings on the Road

Direction Arrows Lane guidance

Arrows painted on the road surface inside a lane indicate the permitted direction(s) for that lane. If an arrow points straight ahead, you must go straight. If it shows a right turn, you must turn right. If your intended direction differs from the arrow, you must change lanes before the arrow.

Arrows are often combined with text (e.g. "BUS" or a lane number) to further clarify the permitted use of that lane.

Speed Limit Markings On the road surface

Speed limit numbers can also be painted directly on the road surface — for example, "30" in a 30-zone or "70" on a rural road. These have the same force as a sign on a pole. They are particularly common at the start of a zone, as a reminder after intersections, or in school zones.

Chapter 3 — What to Take Away

Traffic signs divide into four groups: signs on poles (A through L categories), traffic lights, variable message signs, and road surface markings. The authority hierarchy is: traffic controller > traffic lights > road signs > general rules. A continuous line may not be crossed. A broken line may be crossed when safe. Shark's teeth always mean yield. A red X above a motorway lane means that lane is closed.

For the exam, focus especially on: the B6/B7 distinction (give way vs full stop), the meaning of yellow at traffic lights, the green conditional arrow, matrix signs on motorways, and the difference between solid and broken bicycle lane borders.
