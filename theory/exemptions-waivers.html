Chapter 6

Exemptions & Waivers

When the rules have exceptions — who can grant them, who benefits from them, and what they actually permit.

  • General Exemptions
  • Seatbelt Exemptions
  • Other Waivers
  • Priority Vehicle Exemptions

The traffic rules in Chapters 2 through 5 apply to everyone — but Dutch law also recognises that certain situations, certain vehicles or certain people genuinely need to operate outside those rules. Chapter 6 sets out the legal framework for granting exemptions and waivers: who has the authority to grant them, under what conditions they are valid, and what they permit.

For the theory exam, the most important topics in this chapter are seatbelt exemptions and the special rules that apply to priority vehicles when responding to an emergency.

§ 1 — General Exemptions

The legal basis for granting exemptions — and who has the authority to do so.

What Is an Exemption? Ontheffing

An exemption (ontheffing) is an official decision that releases a specific person, vehicle or category of road user from a specific rule in the RVV 1990. Exemptions are not automatic — they must be applied for and formally granted by the competent authority.

A waiver (vrijstelling) is slightly different: it is a general rule built into the regulations that automatically applies to a defined category, without needing to be individually applied for. Both achieve a similar result — they permit something that would otherwise be prohibited — but through different mechanisms.

Who Grants Exemptions? Competent Authority

The authority to grant exemptions from the RVV 1990 lies with the competent authority (bevoegd gezag). In practice this means:

  • The Minister of Infrastructure and Water Management — for exemptions that apply nationally or to categories that cross municipal boundaries
  • Provincial authorities — for provincial roads
  • Municipal authorities — for roads within their jurisdiction
  • Water boards — for roads they manage

The competent authority may attach conditions to an exemption. These conditions are legally binding — violating a condition of an exemption is equivalent to violating the underlying rule.

Types of Situations That May Warrant an Exemption Common cases

Exemptions are typically granted for situations where strict application of the rules would be unreasonable, disproportionate or impractical. Common categories include:

  • Oversized or overweight loads — vehicles or combinations that exceed normal dimension or weight limits
  • Specialist vehicles — road sweepers, concrete pumps, mobile cranes operating in restricted areas
  • Events and temporary situations — road closures, special routes for races or processions
  • Medical or physical conditions — seatbelt exemptions for people who cannot safely wear one
  • Professional operations — certain trade or construction activities requiring access to restricted roads
Exemption must be carried

When an exemption is granted for a specific vehicle or operation, the exemption document must be carried in the vehicle and shown on request to enforcement officers. A verbal claim of exemption without documentation carries no legal weight.

Exemption is specific

An exemption applies only to the specific rule, vehicle, route or time period stated in the document. It does not grant a general licence to ignore other rules. Only the precisely stated exception is permitted.

Exemptions can be withdrawn

The granting authority can revoke an exemption if conditions are not met, if circumstances change, or if the exemption is misused. Driving on a revoked exemption is a violation.

Automatic waivers exist

Some waivers are built directly into the RVV 1990 and apply automatically without a formal application — for example, the waiver allowing priority vehicles to exceed speed limits when responding to an emergency.

Exemption vs Exception vs Waiver

Dutch traffic law uses three related but distinct concepts. An exemption (ontheffing) is individually granted for a specific case. A waiver (vrijstelling) applies automatically to a defined category and is written into the regulations. An exception (uitzondering) is a built-in rule that limits the scope of another rule — for example, the exception that allows buses to stop at bus stops despite normal stopping restrictions. All three result in permitted behaviour that would otherwise be prohibited.

§ 2 — Seatbelt Exemptions

Who is exempt from the seatbelt obligation — and under what conditions.

The General Rule Autogordel

The general rule is clear: every occupant of a motor vehicle must wear a seatbelt at all times while the vehicle is in motion. The driver is responsible for ensuring passengers under 18 are properly restrained. There are no exceptions based on speed, distance or road type — the seatbelt must always be worn.

However, the RVV 1990 acknowledges that there are genuine situations — medical, physical or operational — where wearing a seatbelt is not possible or not appropriate. For these situations, specific exemptions exist.

Who Is Exempt from Wearing a Seatbelt

🏥
Medical certificate holders

People with a medical condition that makes wearing a seatbelt dangerous or impossible — confirmed by a doctor's certificate — are exempt. The certificate must be carried in the vehicle.

🚗
Driver reversing

A driver who is reversing their vehicle is exempt from the seatbelt requirement for the duration of the reversing manoeuvre only.

🚕
Taxi drivers with passengers

Taxi drivers are exempt from wearing a seatbelt when carrying passengers — because frequent stops, exits and re-entries make continuous seatbelt use impractical.

📦
Delivery drivers in built-up areas

Drivers making frequent short deliveries in a built-up area — such as postal workers or couriers — are exempt during the delivery rounds where stopping is continuous.

🏗️
Certain professional operations

Drivers of certain vehicles used for road maintenance, refuse collection or similar operations that require repeated stopping and exiting may be exempt during those operations.

🚌
Bus passengers standing

Passengers standing in a bus (where seatbelts are not available for standing positions) are naturally exempt — there is no seatbelt to wear in those positions.

The Medical Certificate — Carry It Always

If you are exempt from wearing a seatbelt due to a medical condition, the exemption certificate must be carried in the vehicle at all times and produced immediately upon request by an enforcement officer. Claiming a medical exemption without the certificate is not accepted. The certificate must state clearly that wearing a seatbelt is medically contraindicated and must be signed by a registered doctor.

Child Restraint Exemptions Kinderbeveiligingssysteem

The child restraint obligation — children under 1.35 m must use an approved child seat — also has limited exemptions:

  • In taxis, when no child seat is available and the child is accompanied by an adult — the adult seatbelt may be used instead, and the child must sit in the rear
  • Children with a medical condition preventing use of a child seat — confirmed by a medical certificate
  • In vehicles where fitting a child seat is technically not possible due to the vehicle's design — the child must still travel in the rear using an adult seatbelt
These exemptions are narrow. The default rule — child seat for all children under 1.35 m — applies in virtually all normal driving situations. The exemptions exist only for genuinely exceptional circumstances.

§ 3 & 4 — Other Waivers

Additional waiver categories that appear in the RVV 1990.

Waivers for Specific Road Sections Road authority decisions

Road authorities — municipalities, provinces, the national government — can grant waivers for specific roads or sections that allow vehicles or operations that would otherwise be prohibited. Examples include:

  • Allowing heavy goods vehicles on roads with a weight restriction, for essential deliveries
  • Permitting cyclists on roads where cycling is normally prohibited
  • Allowing access to closed roads for authorised maintenance work
  • Granting time-limited access to pedestrian zones for loading and unloading

These waivers are typically indicated by supplementary boards below the relevant signs, specifying the time, vehicle type or other conditions under which the exception applies.

Waivers for Special Events Events

For organised events — cycling races, marathons, markets, festivals — road authorities can temporarily waive normal traffic rules for specific roads and time windows. These waivers are typically accompanied by temporary signs and traffic controller deployment. Participants in permitted events operate under the conditions of the event waiver, not normal traffic rules, for the duration of the event.

Supplementary Boards as Waiver Indicators

One of the most practical ways waivers appear in everyday traffic is through supplementary boards. A "no entry" sign (C1) with a supplementary board saying "except for residents" is a built-in waiver. A "no parking" sign with hours shown is a time-limited waiver. A bicycle prohibition sign with "except cyclists going straight ahead" is a directional waiver. Always read supplementary boards — they frequently turn a prohibition into a partial permission.

§ 5 — Priority Vehicle Exemptions

The special rules that apply to ambulances, fire trucks, police vehicles and other emergency services when responding to an emergency.

What Is a Priority Vehicle? 🚨 Voorrangsvoertuig

A priority vehicle is a motor vehicle that is simultaneously displaying both a blue flashing or rotating light and a siren. Only when both signals are active does the vehicle qualify as a priority vehicle and benefit from the associated exemptions.

The blue light alone, or the siren alone, does not create a priority vehicle. Both must be active at the same time.

🚨 What a Priority Vehicle May Do

When both blue light and siren are active, the following exemptions automatically apply — no individual permit needed:

Exceed speed limitsDrive faster than the posted limit when necessary to reach an emergency.
Pass red traffic lightsProceed through a red light after ensuring it is safe to do so.
Ignore give-way obligationsProceed at intersections without waiting for right of way.
Use bus lanesDrive in bus lanes and other restricted lanes.
Drive on the wrong sideUse the oncoming lane when the path ahead is blocked.
Stop anywhereStop in locations where stopping is normally prohibited — including yellow lines, bus stops and junctions.
Ignore direction signsProceed in a direction that a sign would normally prohibit.
Use the hard shoulderDrive on the emergency shoulder of a motorway.
Duty of Care Always Remains Critical limit

Even with all these exemptions active, a priority vehicle driver is never exempt from the duty of care. They must always drive in a way that does not needlessly endanger other road users. Exceeding a speed limit does not mean driving recklessly. Passing a red light does not mean driving without looking. The exemptions permit breaking specific rules — they do not permit endangering lives.

A priority vehicle driver who causes an accident through recklessness cannot hide behind the exemptions. The exemptions are tools for efficiency, not a licence for dangerous driving.

This is a frequently tested point in the theory exam: priority vehicles have exemptions from specific rules, but they are never exempt from the fundamental duty of care toward other road users.
Other Road Users — Obligations When a Priority Vehicle Approaches Your duty

When a priority vehicle approaches with both blue light and siren active, every other road user must:

  • Make way immediately — move to the side, slow down, stop if necessary
  • Keep the path clear — do not block the priority vehicle's route
  • Act safely — making way does not mean endangering yourself or others; find the safest way to create space
  • Not follow the priority vehicle — it is illegal to follow a priority vehicle through a red light or into a restricted area simply because the priority vehicle went there
You are obliged to make way — but you are not permitted to break traffic rules yourself while doing so, except to the minimum extent necessary to create a clear path. Moving briefly onto a yellow line to let an ambulance pass is permitted; running a red light because an ambulance passed through is not.
✅ Permitted when making way
  • Stopping briefly on a yellow line
  • Stopping at a bus stop briefly
  • Moving onto the hard shoulder of a motorway to create space
  • Crossing a solid white line to move aside if no safer option exists
🚫 Not permitted when making way
  • Running a red light yourself
  • Following the priority vehicle through a red light
  • Driving into oncoming traffic unsafely
  • Stopping in a way that blocks other emergency access
Which Vehicles Can Be Priority Vehicles? Authorised vehicles

Only vehicles for which blue lights and sirens are officially authorised may become priority vehicles. This includes:

  • Ambulances — when responding to medical emergencies
  • Fire trucks — when responding to fires or other emergencies
  • Police vehicles — when responding to incidents requiring urgent response
  • Other emergency services — officially authorised vehicles of coast guard, bomb disposal, certain military units

A doctor's car with a green flashing light is not a priority vehicle in the legal sense — it has a different, lesser status. Green flashing lights are a request for courtesy, not a legal command to yield.

Green Flashing Light — Not a Priority Vehicle

A green flashing light — used by some doctors and certain other authorised persons — does not create a priority vehicle. Other road users are encouraged to make way out of courtesy, but they are not legally obliged to do so. The legal obligation to yield applies only to vehicles displaying both a blue light and a siren simultaneously. Never confuse a green light with a blue one — the legal consequences are completely different.

Chapter 6 — What to Take Away

Chapter 6 covers the framework of exceptions to the rules. Exemptions are individually granted; waivers apply automatically to defined categories. The competent authority — municipal, provincial or national — grants exemptions, which must be carried and shown on request.

For the exam, focus especially on: seatbelt exemptions (medical certificate, reversing driver, taxi driver, delivery driver), priority vehicle exemptions (both blue light AND siren required), the duty of care that always remains even for priority vehicles, and the fact that other road users must make way but may not themselves break traffic rules beyond what is strictly necessary.

The green flashing light distinction — not a priority vehicle — is a classic exam trap. Remember: blue + siren = priority vehicle. Green light alone = courtesy request only.
