Chapter 4

Instructions from Traffic Controllers

When a human takes charge of traffic — who they are, what their gestures mean, and why their word overrides every sign and light on the road.

  • Obligations of Road Users
  • The Priority Order

Chapter 4 is short but carries significant weight. It establishes two things: what road users must do when a traffic controller is directing traffic, and the precise order of authority among traffic controllers, traffic lights, road signs and the general traffic rules.

Understanding this hierarchy is essential — it explains why a police officer waving you forward at a red light is not asking you to break the law, and why the rules you learned in Chapters 2 and 3 always have a ceiling above them.

§ 1 — Obligations of Road Users

What every road user must do when a traffic controller is present — and what a traffic controller is authorised to do.

Who Is a Traffic Controller? Verkeersregelaar

A traffic controller is a person officially authorised to direct road traffic. This includes:

  • Police officers — always authorised to direct traffic
  • Military police — for military convoys and related situations
  • Officially appointed civilian traffic controllers — for events, roadworks, school crossings and similar situations

Traffic controllers wear high-visibility clothing or uniforms and typically stand at an intersection or crossing point. Their authorisation to direct traffic must be officially granted — a random person waving at drivers does not qualify as a traffic controller in the legal sense.

Obligation to Follow Instructions All road users

Every road user must immediately and completely follow all instructions given by a traffic controller. This is an unconditional obligation — it does not matter whether you agree with the instruction, whether it contradicts a sign, or whether it seems to go against the normal rules. You must comply.

Failure to follow a traffic controller's instruction is a traffic violation, regardless of what other signs or lights indicate.

Instructions Can Override Red Lights Yes, really

One of the most practically important points in this chapter: a traffic controller can instruct you to proceed through a red traffic light. Because the controller's authority is higher than the traffic light, following this instruction is not a violation — it is a legal obligation.

Similarly, a controller can stop you at a green light, direct you to turn where a sign prohibits turning, or hold you where you would normally have right of way. In all these cases, the controller's instruction takes precedence.

Always watch for the controller, not just the lights. If a controller is present, focus primarily on their gestures — the lights become secondary.
Instructions by Gesture — What Each Gesture Means Hand signals

Traffic controllers communicate through standardised arm and hand gestures defined in Annex 2 of the RVV 1990. All road users must be able to recognise and respond to these gestures. The most important ones are shown below.

Arms stretched sideways
Traffic from the front and behind must stop. Traffic from the left and right may proceed.
One arm extended forward
Traffic approaching from the front must stop. The controller's body faces the direction being stopped.
Arm raised vertically
All traffic from all directions must stop immediately. Used to bring all movement to a halt before changing the flow.
Arm waving forward / beckoning
Traffic in the indicated direction may proceed. The controller waves the arm toward themselves or sweeps it in the direction of travel.
Back turned to you
If the controller has their back to you, your direction is stopped. Do not proceed until the controller faces you or gives a proceed signal.
Facing you directly
If the controller faces you and holds their arm out toward you, your direction must stop. Treat this as a red light.
The Core Logic of Controller Gestures

The gestures follow a simple logic: the controller's body divides traffic into two streams — traffic in line with their body, and traffic crossing it. When arms are extended sideways, the traffic running alongside their outstretched arms may go; the traffic facing their front or back must stop. When an arm points at you or is raised toward you, you stop.

Responding Safely to Instructions Practical guidance

When a traffic controller gives you an instruction, respond smoothly and promptly. Do not brake suddenly or swerve — the controller expects you to respond in a controlled manner. If you are unsure of the instruction, reduce speed and make eye contact with the controller for clarification.

Never ignore a controller's signal because a traffic light is showing green. The controller has seen the full picture at that intersection — trust their judgement.

Traffic Controllers at Roadworks Common scenario

Traffic controllers are frequently deployed at roadworks where normal traffic flow is disrupted. They manage alternating one-way sections, guide traffic around equipment, and respond to changing conditions in real time. At a roadworks site, their instructions may change rapidly — stay alert and watch the controller continuously, not just at the moment you first see them.

Traffic Controllers at Events Events & gatherings

At large events — concerts, sports matches, markets — authorised civilian traffic controllers manage vehicle and pedestrian flow around the venue. Their authority is identical to any other official controller. Obey their instructions even if it means taking a longer route or waiting longer than you would normally expect.

§ 2 — The Priority Order of Instructions, Signs and Rules

When different sources of instruction conflict, this order always resolves the question.

The Four Levels of Authority

Dutch traffic law establishes a clear hierarchy of authority. From highest to lowest:

  • Level 1 — Traffic controller: A person officially authorised to direct traffic. Their instructions override everything else.
  • Level 2 — Traffic lights: Red, yellow and green signals. They override road signs and general rules, but a controller can override them.
  • Level 3 — Road signs: Signs on poles, matrix signs, road surface markings. They override the general traffic rules, but lights and controllers can override them.
  • Level 4 — General traffic rules: The rules in Chapter 2 — keep right, give way to the right, speed limits, etc. These apply whenever no higher-level instruction is in place.
Authority Hierarchy

Higher levels always override lower levels. No exceptions.

Controller vs Traffic Light Level 1 wins

A traffic controller's instruction always overrides a traffic light signal. If a controller waves you forward while the light is red — proceed. If a controller holds up their hand while the light is green — stop. The light becomes irrelevant the moment a controller is present and actively directing.

This is not an exception to the red-light rule — it is the rule working exactly as intended. The hierarchy was designed precisely for situations where automatic signals cannot respond to real-time conditions.
Traffic Light vs Road Sign Level 2 wins

A traffic light overrides a road sign. For example: a priority road sign (B1) normally gives you right of way, but if a traffic light at an intersection shows red, you must stop — the light takes precedence over the B1 sign. Similarly, a variable speed limit sign shown on a matrix light overrides any fixed speed limit sign below it.

Road Sign vs General Rule Level 3 wins

A road sign overrides the general traffic rules. The classic example: the general rule is give way to the right. But if a priority road sign (B1) is present, you have right of way — the sign cancels the general rule. If a speed limit sign shows 30 km/h on a road where the default is 50 km/h, the sign applies.

When No Higher Authority Is Present Default: general rules

When there is no traffic controller, no traffic lights, and no relevant road signs — the general traffic rules of Chapter 2 apply in full. This is the baseline. Most roads in the Netherlands operate entirely on these defaults, which is why understanding Chapter 2 thoroughly matters so much.

Non-functioning traffic lights — lights that are completely off or showing no signal — are treated as if no light is present. The general rules then apply, including give way to the right at intersections.

Worked Scenarios — Applying the Hierarchy

🟢
Scenario 1: Controller waves you forward at a red light

What to do: Proceed. The controller's instruction (Level 1) overrides the red light (Level 2). This is legal and correct. Check for crossing traffic as you go — the controller may not have stopped everyone.

🔴
Scenario 2: Traffic light is green but controller holds their hand up toward you

What to do: Stop. The controller (Level 1) overrides the green light (Level 2). Do not proceed even though the light permits it. Wait for the controller to signal you forward.

🟡
Scenario 3: You are on a priority road (B1 sign) but the traffic light shows red

What to do: Stop at the red light. The traffic light (Level 2) overrides the priority road sign (Level 3). The B1 sign gives you priority over crossing traffic at unmarked intersections — but at a signalised intersection, the lights take over.

🔵
Scenario 4: No signs, no lights, no controller — you approach an intersection

What to do: Apply the general rules (Level 4). Give way to drivers approaching from your right. Give way to trams regardless of direction. If on an unpaved road, give way to traffic on a paved road.

⚫
Scenario 5: Traffic lights are completely off — not functioning

What to do: Treat the intersection as uncontrolled. The general traffic rules apply. Give way to the right. Proceed carefully and be prepared for other drivers who may not be aware the lights are off.

🟠
Scenario 6: A matrix sign above your motorway lane shows 90 km/h, but a fixed sign below says 130 km/h

What to do: Drive at 90 km/h. The matrix sign (a variable sign, legally a traffic light in terms of authority — Level 2) overrides the fixed sign (Level 3). Always follow the matrix display.

How to Think About the Hierarchy in Practice

Every time you approach an intersection or enter a new road section, scan in order: Is there a traffic controller present? → follow them. Are there traffic lights? → follow them. Are there road signs? → follow them. Nothing above applies? → apply the general rules. This mental checklist takes seconds and ensures you always respond to the highest-authority instruction available.

Chapter 4 — What to Take Away

Chapter 4 establishes the top of the authority hierarchy: traffic controllers override everything. Their instructions are legally binding for all road users, including cyclists and pedestrians. The hierarchy runs: traffic controller › traffic lights › road signs › general traffic rules. Each level overrides all levels below it, with no exceptions.

For the exam: know the gesture meanings for traffic controllers, understand that a controller can legally instruct you through a red light, and be able to apply the hierarchy to scenarios where two levels conflict. Non-functioning lights revert to general rules — this is a classic exam question.
